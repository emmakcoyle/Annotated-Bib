**Annotation:**

Sharman's *The Art of Anthropology* engages with critical questions surrounding the commodification and romanticization of culture, particularly in the context of the intersection between anthropology and art. The article asks whether one can commodify or romanticize oneself within the context of an art movement, exploring the dynamics of self-representation, particularly through ethnography. This exploration delves into the broader issue of whether it is possible to maintain objectivity when the ethnographer is immersed in the culture they study, questioning if one can be an ethnographer "from within."

Sharman begins by addressing the problematic relationship between art and anthropology, especially in contexts like that of Limon, where the art world often falsifies itself, distorting or idealizing representations of indigenous cultures and practices. This ties into the concept of salvage ethnography — the idea of preserving "authentic" cultural practices before they are lost, often without acknowledging the biases that come with defining "authenticity" from a Western perspective. This framework complicates the notion of cultural preservation and raises the question of what gets left out when individuals from within a culture attempt to save the "good parts" for outsiders. In relation to this, the work challenges the notion that all cultural practices fit neatly within the scope of "tradition" or are captured in their most genuine form for external consumption.

One significant aspect of Sharman's work is the discussion of how national and global art markets shape the recognition of art. In Costa Rica, for instance, the art market legitimates only those artistic practices that conform to the dominant Westernized vision, as noted in the following quotation: "Implicit in Spence's astute grasp of the Costa Rican art world is that while nationally there may be multiple stylistic visions, the economics of the dominant art market will only legitimate art that conforms to the dominant vision." (Sharman, The Art of Anthropology, p. 15). This resonates with Arthur Danto's ideas in *The Artworld*, where the definition of art is influenced by market forces and cultural gatekeepers, determining what is accepted and what is excluded.

Sharman also explores how the role of the artist and ethnographer have converged over time. The article makes an interesting comparison between Paul Gauguin's work in painting and the ethnographic approach to writing. Both artists and anthropologists, as Sharman suggests, "lost interest in 'mere imitation'" and began to consciously produce representations of reality that were not intended to deceive but to create a new, subjective reality. This comparison highlights the blurring of lines between art and ethnography, where the act of representation itself is a creative, transformative process. Sharman draws on this idea, suggesting that just as ethnography creates cultures through writing, art produces new realities through representation.

The role of photography in ethnography is also discussed. Is photography a more objective medium than painting? Sharman contends that despite photography's ability to capture moments more directly, it is not immune to the biases inherent in any form of representation. The distinction between photography and painting in the context of ethnography is complicated by the context in which these images are produced. Photography may appear more accessible to a broader range of people and offer a more direct way of documenting the world, but it still carries the subjective vision of the photographer. In a way, this further blurs the line between anthropologist and artist, as every photographer is influenced by their cultural context and personal biases. Sharman raises pertinent questions about what gets captured, what gets saved, and what gets shown, questioning the role of photography in shaping ethnographic narratives.

Ultimately, Sharman's piece highlights the complex relationship between art and anthropology, examining the ways in which each field has influenced the other, often unknowingly, and how both face challenges in representing the world accurately and ethically. The article also questions how Western art markets have shaped the ways in which we view and validate cultural practices and works of art, emphasizing the role of commodification in art and the politics of cultural representation.

Painters lost interest in 'mere imitation,' and turned more consciously to producing a bit of reality themselves, specifically intended not to deceive. In this way, Gauguin's innovative approach to painting is the aesthetic equivalent to ethnography's innovative approach to writing." (Sharman, The Art of Anthropology, p. 16)
