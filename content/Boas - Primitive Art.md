**Annotation:**

In *Primitive Art*, Franz Boas lays the groundwork for an anthropological approach to aesthetics by rejecting hierarchical comparisons between Western and non-Western art. One of his central arguments is the deep interrelationship between technical skill and aesthetic perception. As he writes, "a perfect standard of form can be sustained only in a highly developed and perfectly controlled technique," suggesting that what one culture may deem "beautiful" is inseparable from the material conditions and technical practices through which objects are made (p. 11). For Boas, the perception of beauty is not an innate or universal standard but rather an emergent property of learned skills, techniques, and cultural context.

His discussion of the "stability of form of objects" (p. 11) further reflects his commitment to understanding how form evolves within a cultural system. This phrase signals his interest in repetition, pattern, and constraint as they operate within traditional art forms — not as signs of stagnation, but as evidence of cultural coherence and internal logic. Boas resists Western tendencies to see "primitive" art as chaotic or purely expressive, instead emphasizing its rigorous internal consistency and developed sense of form.

The two selected quotes reflect Boas's larger ethnographic methodology: a close reading of form as culturally embedded and technically mediated. These insights remain foundational for later anthropologists of art, particularly those interested in ethnoaesthetics and the relationship between making, materiality, and perception.
