**Annotation:**

In *The Aesthetic Experience*, Jacques Maquet approaches art not through cultural relativism or symbolic decoding, but through phenomenology — asking what it feels like to look at an artwork. His interest lies in the perceptual, embodied dimensions of aesthetic experience rather than in sociological meaning or contextual interpretation. This approach situates the viewer's "personal repository of art experiences" as central to meaning-making, emphasizing that the perception of art is shaped by prior encounters and is deeply subjective.

The specific passage — "The object is seen as a whole, in its totality; its different parts may be perceived as distinct, but are not analyzed separately" — underscores a fundamental move away from dissecting or decoding an artwork's components (a practice common in both formalist criticism and anthropological semiotics). Instead, Maquet argues for a holistic, felt encounter with the visual, prioritizing the aesthetic gaze over critical or interpretive analysis. This resonates with Susan Sontag's call for an "erotics of art" and with Gell's notion of art as a system of affective and relational encounters.

For curatorial and anthropological practices, Maquet's text is essential in reminding us that viewers engage with art not solely through cognition or cultural knowledge but through immediate, embodied perception. His perspective is especially valuable when working across cultural boundaries, where symbolic meanings may not easily translate, but aesthetic encounters — of color, shape, texture, scale — remain available to all. His phenomenological stance also challenges the dominance of Euro-American art theory, proposing instead that aesthetic experience is not universal in form, but in the potential for relational seeing.

**Quotation(s):**

"By referring to one's personal repository or art experiences one may capture the specificity of looking at art objects, and by a phenomenological analysis, one may express in everyday language some characteristics of the visual relationship to art. The object is seen as a whole, in its totality; its different parts may be perceived as distinct, but are not analyzed separately." (Maquet, The Aesthetic Experience, p. 30 or 163 — verify)
