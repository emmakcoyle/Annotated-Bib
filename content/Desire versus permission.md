*Essay · 24hr Neon Mag · 2019*

My lover (let’s call him Rob) received a text message the other day in which his best friend (Bob) said that his lover (Shelly) gave him permission (allowed) him to share nude pictures of her with my lover (Rob). (I hope you can follow that doozy of a sentence as I try to keep people’s real names private.) The pictures were lovely and I couldn’t help but think of Eve standing in the garden fully nude before she had any sense of societal expectations regarding nudity, shame, or propriety. Something about the exchange struck me though and I began to wonder if the pictures that I saw were something she desired to share with people or if they were something she allowed (gave permission) to share with people. Does it make a difference when it comes to consent? Is permission the same as desire?

I would like to be clear that the rest of the essay is hypothetical and does not directly reflect on the truth of the above instance. To the best of my knowledge, that original instance was in fact consensual, desired, and approved by all parties. Everything that I ponder throughout the rest of the essay is not about any real life people except for using them as a jumping off point to explore (what has unexpectedly become much more about) the ethics of consent then I originally thought would be. With that disclaimer out of the way...let’s get back to it!

Shelly has never met Rob, may never meet Rob, and yet she has agreed to (or decided to) have her nude pictures shared with him. She is going purely off her trust in Bob that Rob isn't going to do anything nefarious with those images or abuse the privilege in any way. I tend to think that privately sharing nudes, not on social media platforms, has more weight to it. It would be like posting a story to facebook versus telling someone a story. There are different expectations and a level of intimacy when there are direct person to person interactions.

I would also like to note that she was not the one distributing the images, Bob was distributing them. She did not have control over what restrictions or qualifications would be included with the images. She was not able to make sure they were not shared further or for that matter she wasn't able to ensure that they were shared further. Did she enjoy giving up her control over the images? Was that erotic to her? Simply something she was ok with, shrugging her shoulders with an eh why not?

Most of this so far goes back to the question: “does acquiescence constitute a form of consent?” (*The Ethics of Consent: Theory and Practice*, Miller) and much like John Kleinig’s footnote in his *Nature of Consent*, “I am inclined to say that it depends.” (*The Ethics of Consent: Theory and Practice*, Miller). He goes on to say that “where consent is called for and refusal to consent is easily signified then acquiescence might reasonably be taken to constitute consent. But in cases in which it might be costly to refuse consent, acquiescence may not be taken to signify consent.” (*The Ethics of Consent: Theory and Practice*, Miller). All of that is really just to say that if you think saying no is going to cost you in some way, then agreeing isn't real consent. We might even consider it coercion.

What are the stakes involved in something existing in the nebulous grey area of expectations and interpersonal relationships? For the above example with Shelly, Rob, and Bob, is there an existent pressure to either give permission for private images to be shared or a sense of obligation? Merely the act of being asked can pressure a person to give consent with the fear of what saying no will mean to a possibly nebulous relationship. *The Ethics of Consent* says that in “in appropriate circumstances, even offers may be coercive”, and if that's the case then I don't see why a request wouldn’t have more potential for affecting a person’s choice.

I would also like to note that Bob has in his possession a series of nude photos of Shelly. Even if she enjoyed the images being taken, think they look really pretty, and totally wanted them shared with Rob and whoever else, Bob still has these images. At this moment in time, photos of that nature being seen publicly, can affect your job prospects, your personal relationships, your familial relationships, and all sorts of other things that you can probably Google if you wanted to get more details. This means that without ever discussing it or intending it, Bob now has a lot of leverage against Shelly which it would be difficult to imagine her not being aware of.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy asks “How much do “implicit threats” count as threats?” and while it is mostly referencing medical consent between a Doctor and a Patient, it does a great job explaining why coercion doesn't have to be explicit or intentional to invalidate consent. It is the inherent power structure in the relationship and its inequality that make (even unfounded) fear in the patient, that their care would suffer for not consenting, a problem which invalidates consent. I’m going to include the original example for you here because I think it does a better job explaining than I do: “Imagine a practitioner who asks her own patient to participate in a study that the practitioner runs, and the patient fears that care would suffer if he declines, without the practitioner actually saying so. When the patient’s fear is well-founded, or even intentionally instilled by the practitioner, the norm has been to consider consent invalid. This clearly makes sense if what makes coercion problematic is (unjust) curtailment of options. However, even when the fear is unfounded and unintended, the hierarchy and the power inequality of the physician-patient relationship are often still thought to make such consent involuntary. The thought is that someone’s options count in this context as curtailed even when what curtails them is uncertainty and the patient’s own pessimism.” -(*The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, [plato.stanford.edu/entries/informed-consent/#Coe](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/informed-consent/#Coe))

As we got into earlier, Bob has a lot of leverage against Shelly, and because of that and because “at present, notwithstanding their formal civic status, women are regarded as men’s ‘natural’ subordinates and hence as incapable of consent” since consent can only be given between equals, and the power balance between Bob and Shelly is most certainly unequal (*Women and Consent*, Carole Pateman). Perhaps the woman was not even knowingly evaluating the repercussions for her consent or non-consent but a relationship that exists outside of an established contract would give her no social power to assert her rights in the event that she was anticipating repercussions for her entitled non-consent. There is no way that she could righteously hold him accountable if, for instance, her lack of consent influenced his decisions to keep in touch with her or not. Within that context there would be an undue pressure to agree to his request. He wouldn’t even have to threaten or imply that that was a possibility. She would just have to be subconsciously aware that her actions could be seen as “uncool” so that consent would in fact be invalid.

There have been numerous think-pieces and articles written about the “cool girl” stereotype and the effect that it has on women. In summary, it is written that to maintain desirability in the eyes of a person we are interested in, a woman swallows her discomfort (*Why I’m so over being the cool girl when it comes to relationships*, Fashion Slave). People want to be liked so they avoid doing things that will label them as “crazy” which is a term historically used against women when they assert their boundaries.

Let's take a look at another example that I think will give a bit more insight into all that stuff between Rob, Bob, and Shelly. A friend of mine asked to have her birthday party at a mutual friend’s co-op. Some people there are allowing the party to happen because it would be more of an inconvenience to not allow it instead of what we would probably call enthusiastic consent or desire. They accept that it will happen and are at peace with it but it is doubtful that they really want that party happening at their house. It is sometimes easier to consent to something than deal with the consequences of enforcing your own boundaries. Which is the same as if Shelly tried to enforce boundaries. Perhaps it would be more effort to enforce them than to concede them. *The Ethics of Consent* would argue that that isn’t true consent.

“It is difficult to conceive of a moral code, especially within a civilized society, without some recognition to the requirement and moral force of consent. People simultaneously have an interest in control over their bodies and possessions and seek to engage in cooperative acuities with others on terms that the cooperating parties can mutually accept. A requirement of consent, from a moral perspective, protects people from unauthorized invasions of their bodies and property. In addition to its protective function, consent is a facilitative moral power. Our consent makes interpersonal conduct permissible that would otherwise be prohibited as wrongful. And through our capacity to undertake obligations or bind oneself, consent makes possible cooperative acuities in which one person must perform before another and also allows us to create expectations about one’s future behavior. It is fair to say that modern liberal-democratic societies have been characterized by an ever-growing domain of personal sovereignty, making consent salient across a wide swath of human activities, including sexual relations, employment, medical care, buying and selling, medical research, professional relationships, and so forth. The centrality of consent to our moral and legal lives is not matched by philosophical attention to the theory of consent. There is an extensive literature on the ethics of consent that is confined to one context or another, but few general treatments. For example, at least as far back as Plato and continuing through Locke (and beyond), it has been argued that citizens of (some) societies have an obligation to obey the law because they consent to do so. Other thinkers, starting at least with Hume, have contested that claim. Inspired in part by the feminist movement, there are now numerous treatments of consent to sexual relations. While not always couched in the language of consent, the entire corpus of contract law revolves around consent.”- (*The Ethics of Consent: Theory and Practice*, Miller)

Additionally Pateman asks: “if ‘no,’ when uttered by a woman, is to be reinterpreted as ‘yes,’ then all the comfortable assumptions about her “consent” are also thrown into disarray. Why should a woman’s ‘yes’ be more privileged, be any the less open to invalidation, than her ‘no’?” (*Women and Consent*, Carole Pateman). I mean, we have all, in person or in the movies, heard or seen a woman being “convinced” to consent to behavior or sexual advances. Just think about any movie featuring Bond, James Bond. But being convinced to consent to an act doesn't have to be as blatant as that. While reading *Women and Consent*, along with some other sources, I kept running into the idea that men are often not found legally responsible for rape if they (even misinformedly) claim that they thought they had consent. I’m not trying to talk about rape right now, becasue this is a very different branch of the consent tree, but I am interested in the idea that one is not responsible for their actions if they think they acted in good faith.

So getting back to the original example, it would be completely reasonable for her to act on the expectation that her consent or non consent would change the terms of the relationship and without a previously existing social contract (clearly defined relationship) her choosing to have those images sent, without direct control of the distribution, would be influenced by these “compulsions that affect what [one] agrees to” (*The Ethics of Consent: Theory and Practice*, Miller) including the preservation of the preexisting power balance. But that would also be disregarding the possibility of the eroticisation of permission. To desire to give permission and nothing more. And on reflection, that is a type of power.

Look to Hegel for more on desire as power. “For Hegel, we can be aware of ourselves only when others are conscious of us” (*Philosophy and Desire*, Silverman). By giving her permission, Shelly is exerting a certain type of power, in line with the way royalty has, in the past, given their permission: above excitement, above enthusiasm, and, with a wave of their hand, they can allow anything, be it people entering their presence or a marriage between nobility. To be enthusiastic, to show desire, is in a way a submission. It shows where a person's emotions lie, it is vulnerable to openly desire. “Augustine’s problem with desire is that it could lead to a surrender to bodily interests. Desire that is anything other than desire for a *visio Dei* is misguided. And so desire is distinguished from will.” (*Philosophy and Desire*, Silverman)

When I was telling my lover (Rob) about this essay, partially because he would be featured in it and partially because I wanted some feedback on the ideas I had been developing, he told me about an instance I had forgotten from months ago. Rob reminded me that before he had left for a trip I had sent him nudes, saying he could share them with anyone he wanted. Honestly, it sounds like me. Which is probably why I forgot it. I’m the person who made a powerpoint of nudes set to the most disconcerting music I could find and sent it out. Rob and I spoke about that and tried to find the difference between the two examples, mine and Shelly’s, and the only major difference was that I had been in control of the original distribution, I would almost consider myself the copyright holder of the images. At the same time both Shelly and I both clearly got something out of the knowledge that our images were shared.

Tim Themi says that “for Bataille ‘these interminable millennia correspond with man’s slow shaking-off of his original animal nature,’ how ‘he emerged from it by working, by understanding his own mortality and by moving imperceptibly from unashamed sexuality to sexuality with shame, which gave birth to eroticism’”. (*Bataille and the Erotics of the Real*, Tim Themi). Existing in the grey area between simple permission and desire is an eroticisation of consent. I would argue that we could call that enthusiastic permission, instead of the oft spoke of enthusiastic consent, which is the best teaching model for sexual practices but doesn't address the range of interactions that people actually experience. Think of sex workers: they consent, but do they have to be excited and enthusastic about doing their job? Permission can be the intentional masking of desire in a consensual eroticised space, much like the consent involved in BDSM relationships and sexual interactions. Both parties have to be conscious of the boundaries before exploring the power dynamics of enthusiastic permission. Bob has to understand the extent that he can distribute the nudes, they have to have an established trust between them, and he has to have a clear understanding of what permission means in an eroitisized space.

Legally, can Shelly consent? Perhaps not in the strict legal rules laid out above, but in a realistic world, consent is possible and permission can be power.
