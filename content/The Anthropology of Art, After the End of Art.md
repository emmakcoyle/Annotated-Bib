**Annotation:**

This article surveys anthropological approaches to art, emphasizing the discipline's persistent questioning of what constitutes "art" across cultures and institutions. Central to the discussion is the tension between critically examining the art category itself—what Alfred Gell termed "methodological philistinism"—and the simultaneous desire to produce aesthetic and political effects that resonate with new publics. The authors argue that anthropology's contribution lies in its capacity to explore how objects are transformed into "art" through social processes involving commerce, criticism, and curatorial practice (p. 321, 324).

The article situates contemporary anthropological studies of art within broader conversations about agency, materiality, and community collaboration. Citing Jason Jackson's work, the authors describe how anthropologists are attuned to the incomplete nature of social life embedded in artworks, particularly through the lens of human–thing assemblages (p. 325). This anthropological reflexivity informs how scholars and practitioners interpret the making, circulation, and reception of art.

Furthermore, the piece explores experimental, community-based, and participatory art practices that often sit outside mainstream institutions. These practices, while at times critiqued for their perceived inaccessibility (Errington 2012), are presented as meaningful collaborations that question hierarchies between high and low art and contribute to evolving notions of professionalization in both art and anthropology (p. 325).

Another key focus is the entanglement between anthropology and art criticism, where questions of audience become crucial. The blending of these interpretive practices suggests an expanded field in which anthropologists can actively participate in the creation, analysis, and curation of art (p. 326).

The article concludes by highlighting the field of design anthropology, which draws on cultural knowledge to inform systems and aesthetics in both corporate and communal settings. By decoupling aesthetic experience from institutionalized notions of "high art," scholars like Young (2011) expand the terrain of ethnoaesthetics and demonstrate how everyday objects—such as Aboriginal customized cars—can carry symbolic and expressive depth (p. 327). Design anthropology thus reveals the capacity of material culture to disrupt, reframe, and reconceive artistic value beyond canonical forms.
