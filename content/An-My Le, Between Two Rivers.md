## **An-My Lê: Between Two Rivers** at MoMA
## Notes and Reflections 



*Reformatted with help from AI*

  

### Core Themes:

  

War beyond war: The exhibition challenges traditional conceptions of war, moving away from the singular image of violent combat and instead exploring "false wars": border disputes, ecological collapse, social unrest, and psychological fragmentation.

  

These are wars without uniforms — wars of everyday violence, invisible trauma, and cultural performance.

  

### Key Questions Raised:

  

1. What constitutes war? The show pushes back against binary ideas of peace vs. war, showing how landscapes themselves become palimpsests of conflict — disrupted, layered, re-inscribed.
    

  

Her work can be seen as a visual response to the "absence" of war imagery—instead, it points to what is left behind, hidden, buried, or performed.

  

2. Relation to Aristotle – [[Aesthetics, Catharsis, Aristotle 2024|Mimesis]] and Beauty Aristotle believed in mimesis as imitation grounded in human perception — a way to learn, to find [[Aesthetics, Catharsis, Aristotle 2024|catharsis]], and to find truth through representation.
    

  

In this show, layers of imitation appear: imitation of landscape, of ruins, of uniforms, of borders. But also imitation of "peace" and "reality."

  

Is Ah-Mi-Lee inverting [[Aesthetics, Catharsis, Aristotle 2024|Aristotle]]? Using imitation to show the artificiality of war's imagery rather than revealing a universal truth?

  

Beauty in this work is ambiguous — the photos are stunning, rich in texture and tone — but that very beauty can be disturbing. Is this a case where beauty draws us in, only to expose the unease beneath the surface?

  

3. The Role of Interpretation These photographs demand interpretation — they do not "speak for themselves."
    

  

This seems to challenge Susan [[Sontag, Susan. "Against Interpretation."|Sontag]]'s claim that photographs shouldn't require commentary — that the image alone should suffice.

  

Sontag warned against the numbing effect of endless images of suffering, but Ah-Mi-Lee offers a counter: war here is abstracted, embedded in the land and body, and thus requires explanation to reveal its hidden violence.

  

4. Ethnography and Criticism There's an ethnographic lens in her work: landscapes are studied like fieldnotes, visual evidence of broader systems of control and trauma.
    

  

Yet there's also criticism, in the framing, composition, and aesthetic choices — a deep awareness of the viewer's role and the politics of looking.

  

The embroidery elements reinforce this — they slow down the process of viewing. Each stitch is a form of labor, a reminder of the body behind the image. They embody interpretation layered on top of photography — not just decoration, but disruption.

  

### Ethical Grounding

  

You note: "Make sure that all interpretation is grounded in the physical tangible aspects of the art."

  

Yes: each interpretation must stem from what is there — the visual evidence, the materials, the marks of the artist. Don't abstract beyond what the image provides.

  

### Questions for Further Exploration:

  

Is this art a new form of visual ethnography? What happens when the subject and interpreter are the same person?

  

Can imitation reveal truth even when it's layered with unreality?

  

How do her personal experiences in Vietnam shape her visual world — is it biography as method?

  

Where is the line between aesthetic beauty and political critique, and can one enhance the other?
