**Annotation:**

In her seminal essay "Against Interpretation," Susan Sontag critiques the dominance of interpretation in modern aesthetics, particularly the impulse to excavate hidden meanings rather than attend to the sensual, formal surface of the work itself. This is especially relevant when thinking about ancient or "primitive" art, which is often approached with the assumption of a "fall from grace" — as if these works originally carried pure meaning that we've since lost or must recover. Sontag calls for a shift away from this kind of interpretive violence, advocating instead for an "erotics of art" that foregrounds experience over explanation. Her argument resists the melancholic tendency to treat ancient art as a puzzle whose truth lies in a lost origin, and instead urges readers to confront the artwork on its own formal and sensory terms.

Though she doesn't cite Barthes directly, Sontag's approach prefigures the "death of the author" — she de-centers the artist's intended meaning and instead centers the viewer's experience of the work's presence. Her rejection of overinterpretation aligns closely with anti-hermeneutic tendencies in contemporary anthropology and curatorial practice, where the goal is not to speak for the work but to create the conditions in which it can speak for itself — or not speak at all, but simply exist as a sensorial encounter.

Her call to "recover our senses" is a potent reminder for those studying material culture or engaging in exhibition-making: the work of art should not be reduced to a message but encountered as an active, affective presence. In this sense, Sontag is deeply aligned with anthropological projects that seek to unsettle the binary between meaning and form, text and object, or viewer and artwork.
