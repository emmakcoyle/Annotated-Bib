[^4]: **On emerge**
	
	Immerse and emerge share the root _mergere_. To immerse is to go in, to emerge is to come out — but crucially, what emerges is not what went in. The word already encodes transformation as its structure. The pageant might be thought of as a technology of emergence in this sense: you enter it as a discrete individual with a discrete relationship to the river, the insects, the other people around you, and you leave as something that has been changed by contact with all of those things. The dragonflies don't follow you home but the attentional practice might. What does the pageant produce? Not just an experience but a capacity. Emergence as the thing immersion generates — and emergence as the word that lets you talk about what comes after, what the practice is for, what it changes in the participant who walks back out into ordinary life. This also opens onto Boal more cleanly: the spect-actor doesn't stay in the theater. They emerge.
	
	  
	
	**On consent and risk**
	
	Water immerses you whether you want it to or not. You can drown in it. The hydrocommons doesn't ask permission — you are already in it, already made of it, already cycling through it whether you identify with that or not. This is part of what makes Neimanis's argument political rather than merely poetic: the intermingling of bodies, histories, and violences in the hydrocommons is not optional. You cannot insulate yourself from it by refusing to notice it. The pageant operates similarly. You are in the field, the dragonflies arrive, the breath of the crowd attracts the mosquitoes, and this happens whether any individual participant intended it or not. Immersion here is not a choice the audience makes — it is the condition of their presence. That's different from the immersive theater tradition, which tends to frame immersion as a technique applied to a willing participant, an experience you opt into. The pageant and the river are making a different claim: that immersion is not a technique but a condition, and that the question is only whether you become aware of it. This is also where the political edge of the work lives — not in themed content about ecology, but in the formal insistence that you were never outside the system you thought you were observing.
	
	  
	
	**On collage as method**
	
	Stewart describes the rehearsal structure as collage: groups that work separately, that don't see each other's contributions until the day of the pageant, that arrive at a whole through juxtaposition rather than integration. This is also what you are doing with your sources. Neimanis and Latour and Haraway and Boal and Gross don't share a conversation in the literature — you are placing them next to each other and letting the contact generate something neither of them produces alone. The pageant doesn't direct its components into a unified vision, and neither does this project. The form of the inquiry mirrors the form of the object. This could be made explicit as a methodological claim: that a collage method is appropriate to an object that is itself collage, that the dissolved boundaries between your theoretical sources enact the dissolved boundaries the pageant performs. This is also a defense of the project's form against the charge that it lacks a single governing framework. It doesn't lack one. The pageant is the framework, and the pageant is collage.
	
	  
	
	**On dissolution as the political act**
	
	Currently the insurrection argument rests on the puppet as a signifying object that can say what the person cannot — Schumann's formulation. That's a good argument but it's about content: the puppet speaks truth to power. The stronger claim available to you is formal rather than content-based. If immersion dissolves the boundary between self and environment, between performer and audience, between human and more-than-human, then the political act is not what the puppet says but what the pageant does to the conditions of separation that hierarchy requires. Hierarchy needs discrete, bounded subjects who can be ranked. The pageant temporarily produces a different kind of subject — one who has been in the river, breathed with the crowd, moved beside the stilt walkers, rehearsed a form of attention that doesn't prioritize the self. Separation is what makes domination legible. Immersion makes it, briefly, impossible. The insurrection is not in the content of the performance. It is in the form. The puppets are insurrectionists not because of what they say but because of what standing before them does to your sense of your own separateness. This connects your Latour and Haraway and Boal into a single claim: that the pageant is a political form because it is an immersive form, and immersion is political because it dissolves the bounded individual subject that hierarchy depends on.
